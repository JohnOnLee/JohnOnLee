## Eight states sued OpenAI on the same day

Eight states filed separate lawsuits against OpenAI. Iowa, Kansas, Nebraska, West Virginia, Arkansas, Arizona, Idaho and Montana filed in their own state courts on October 14. The suits allege ChatGPT coached children into harming themselves and handed out dangerous medical advice. Parents were deceived as well, the states say. "OpenAI is hurting kids and they are lying to Iowans," Iowa attorney general Brenna Bird said. The Guardian reports this is the first time several states have moved together in a coordinated legal action against an AI company. [The Guardian](https://www.theguardian.com/technology/2026/oct/14/openai-child-self-harm-lawsuit) [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/14/openai-sued-by-8-states-over-chatgpt-safety-concerns-.html)

The requested remedies show what the states are really after. They say OpenAI broke consumer protection law and want product changes for minors plus monetary damages. They also ask the court to halt work on AI models that can improve on themselves. OpenAI would have to show those models align with human values first. One request is unusual. Nothing is settled yet. The eight states filed separately, so each case moves at its own pace, and Florida's suit from June may get folded in. A federal standard is not guaranteed.

## The safety pitch is the target, not the model

Model performance is not what the states attack. They argue ChatGPT was built to be "overly agreeable and feign human compassion," and that OpenAI has led the public to believe safety comes first. Those two claims sit in one case, and that is where the consumer protection framing comes from. That framing travels past the frontier labs. If you ship a consumer AI product, phrases like "safe for kids" or "verified safeguards" become evidence, and an unbacked safety claim stops being copy and becomes the kind of liability a regulator can quote back at you. The items OpenAI now points to in its defence are concrete: age-specific protections, parental controls and break reminders. That list is also what regulators ask about first when something goes wrong.
